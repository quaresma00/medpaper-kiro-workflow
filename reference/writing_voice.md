# Writing voice: confident, not defensive (engine 1.24.0)

**Organise the narrative, never select the evidence.** Lead with the strongest pre-specified
finding and say plainly what the study adds. Every pre-specified outcome and analysis, every
negative result (estimate, 95% CI, plain conclusion) and every necessary caveat stays in the
paper. Each is said once, specifically, in its proper place.

## Rules for the writer (W) and the medical editor (E), with one example each

W1 Say what the study adds, once, at the end of the Introduction and at the start of the
Discussion (population, design or scale). Use "first" or "novel" only when the feasibility
literature search found no comparable study.
- Bad: "To the best of our knowledge, this novel study is the first to explore this topic."
- Good: "We examined this association in 412 000 adults followed for a median of 9 years."

W2 Report a non-significant result as an estimate with its CI and a plain conclusion.
- Bad: "Mortality showed a trend toward significance (P = 0.07)."
- Good: "We found no clear difference in mortality (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.76-1.01); the
  interval was compatible with a small benefit or no effect."

W3 Give each sensitivity or subgroup analysis one purpose clause in Methods; do not argue at
length about analyses that were not done.
- Good: "To assess the influence of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values."

W4 Limitations: one paragraph, three to five specific items, each with the likely direction
of bias. No "Our study has several limitations" opener, no swinging between weakness and
strength. The abstract keeps at most one essential design caveat; the conclusion keeps none.
- Bad: "This study has several limitations. However, it also has strengths. However, ..."
- Good: "Exposure was self-reported, which probably biased the estimates towards the null."

W5 State a necessary caveat once, where it belongs; no prebuttals.
- Bad: "We emphasize that we do not claim causality, and the results should be interpreted
  with caution."
- Good (once, in the limitations paragraph): "As an observational study, it cannot establish
  that the drug causes the event."

E1 Delete prebuttals, "for a fair comparison", empty summaries ("Taken together, these
findings demonstrate ...") and self-weakening adverbs (unfortunately, merely, disappointingly).
- Bad: "Unfortunately, the effect was merely modest." Good: "The effect was modest (OR 1.12)."

E2 One caveat per claim; a repeated caveat stays only in the limitations paragraph.

E3 End the sentence instead of adding ", highlighting ..."; write "is" for "serves as"; keep
one name for one population. Reports, cases and patients are different units of analysis and
are never interchanged.
- Bad: "Statins reduced events, highlighting their role." Good: "Statins reduced events."
- Bad: "Troponin serves as a marker." Good: "Troponin is a marker."

E4 Keep-list test before deleting anything: would removing it mislead a reader? Never delete
the only causal, signal or design caveat (for example, that a disproportionality signal does
not measure incidence or risk). Normal transitions with a citation ("Prior studies have
shown ... [ref]", "Moreover", "In contrast") stay.

Conclusion: reinforce the main message, add no new limitation, and keep the claim as strong
as the design allows (an association stays an association).

## Ideas not adopted, and why

The source idea (a launch, not a work report) also suggests: build the story only around
strengths; drop a metric the study does not win; re-select the main result; delete or weaken
unfavourable experiments; do not volunteer weaknesses before the contribution is clear. In
clinical research these are spin and outcome switching. They conflict with pre-specified
primary outcomes and limitation reporting (CONSORT, STROBE, RECORD, ICMJE), so the engine
rejects them: the primary outcome and analysis are the pre-specified ones, post hoc analyses
are labelled exploratory, negative results are reported, and limitations are stated.
