# Writing voice: confident, not defensive (engine 1.24.0; 1.25.1)

**Organise the narrative, never select the evidence.** Lead with the main question and its answer: the
primary outcome comes first in the Abstract results, the Results and the Discussion's principal-findings
paragraph, whatever its direction. Emphasis may go to the most informative pre-specified findings, but a
positive secondary or post hoc result never displaces or outranks a null primary result; post hoc results
are labelled exploratory. Say plainly what the study adds. Every pre-specified outcome and analysis, every
negative result (estimate, 95% CI, plain conclusion) and every necessary caveat stays in the paper, said
once, specifically, in its proper place.

## What a medical paper must keep (1.25.1)

K1 Null and unfavourable results are reported with the same prominence and format as favourable ones:
estimate, CI, plain conclusion.
- Good: "The primary outcome did not differ (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.85-1.11). Hospitalisation, a secondary
  outcome, was less frequent (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.70-0.94)."
K2 Uncertainty is expressed by intervals and stated assumptions, not by hedging words.
- Bad: "Results might possibly suggest a potential benefit." Good: "The interval was compatible with a 15%
  reduction or an 11% increase."
K3 Limitations are stated plainly - no self-weakening, no repetition - but they are always present: one
paragraph, specific items, and the essential caveat of the design (for example, a disproportionality
signal is not incidence or risk and is affected by reporting biases). The engine checks that every
pre-specified outcome is reported with its interval, that the primary outcome leads, and that the
limitations paragraph is present; an edit that breaks one of these is refused.

## Rules for the writer (W) and the medical editor (E), with one example each

W1 Lead with the main question and its answer (the primary outcome first, whatever its direction). Say
what the study adds, once, at the end of the Introduction and at the start of the Discussion. Use "first"
or "novel" only when the feasibility literature search found no comparable study.
- Bad: "To the best of our knowledge, this novel study is the first to explore this topic."
- Good: "We examined this association in 412 000 adults followed for a median of 9 years."
W2 Report a non-significant result as an estimate with its CI and a plain conclusion.
- Bad: "Mortality showed a trend toward significance (P = 0.07)."
- Good: "We found no clear difference in mortality (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.76-1.01); the interval was
  compatible with a small benefit or no effect."
W3 Give each sensitivity or subgroup analysis one purpose clause in Methods; do not argue at length about
analyses that were not done. Good: "To assess the influence of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values."
W4 Limitations: one paragraph, three to five specific items, each with the likely direction of bias. No
"Our study has several limitations" opener, no swinging between weakness and strength. The abstract keeps
at most one essential design caveat; the conclusion keeps none.
- Bad: "This study has several limitations. However, it also has strengths. However, ..."
  Good: "Exposure was self-reported, which probably biased the estimates towards the null."
W5 State a necessary caveat once, where it belongs; no prebuttals.
- Bad: "We emphasize that we do not claim causality, and the results should be interpreted with caution."
- Good (once, in the limitations paragraph): "As an observational study, it cannot establish that the
  drug causes the event."
E1 Delete prebuttals, "for a fair comparison", empty summaries ("Taken together, these findings
demonstrate ...") and self-weakening adverbs (unfortunately, merely, disappointingly).
- Bad: "Unfortunately, the effect was merely modest." Good: "The effect was modest (OR 1.12)."

E2 One caveat per claim; a repeated caveat stays only in the limitations paragraph.
E3 End the sentence instead of adding ", highlighting ..."; write "is" for "serves as"; keep one name for
one population. Reports, cases and patients are different units of analysis and are never interchanged.
- Bad: "Statins reduced events, highlighting their role." Good: "Statins reduced events."
E4 Keep-list test before deleting anything: would removing it mislead a reader? Never delete or soften a
null or unfavourable result, an interval, a limitation or the only causal, signal or design caveat.
Normal transitions with a citation ("Prior studies have shown ... [ref]", "Moreover") stay.
Conclusion: reinforce the main message, add no new limitation, and keep the claim as strong as the design
allows (an association stays an association).

## Ideas absorbed, kept and rejected

Absorbed from the source idea (a launch, not a work report): no work-report narration (what was done step
by step), a clear argument, the contribution stated plainly, claims aligned with the evidence. Kept, because a medical paper must report them: unfavourable and null results, limitations and the
uncertainty of every estimate.

## Ideas not adopted

Build the story only around strengths; let the strongest result lead in place of the primary outcome;
drop a metric the study does not win; re-select the main result; delete or weaken unfavourable
experiments; do not volunteer weaknesses. In clinical research these are spin and outcome switching and
conflict with pre-specified outcomes and limitation reporting (CONSORT, STROBE, RECORD, ICMJE).
